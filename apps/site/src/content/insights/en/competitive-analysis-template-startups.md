On November 7, 2026, I gave several workshop teams the same assignment: pick a competitor, reconstruct how its website changed, and explain what their own product should do next. The teams that produced the most useful work did not collect the most screenshots. They chose the most comparable company and ended with a decision.

| Step | Question to answer | Evidence to collect | Output |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relevance | Why is this company comparable now? | Customer, stage, business model | One-sentence selection reason |
| History | What changed before and after early traction? | Dated website snapshots | Evolution table |
| Commercial model | How did the offer and pricing change? | Pricing and CTA snapshots | Offer hypothesis |
| Distribution | Which channels appear repeatedly? | Official profiles and referral data | One channel test |
| Decision | What should we do differently? | Evidence above | 14-day experiment |

## What makes a competitor relevant to an early-stage startup?

A relevant competitor is not simply the largest company in your category. It is a company whose earlier customer, business model, and problem are close enough to your current situation that its decisions can teach you something.

Use three comparison roles:

1. **Stage-matched direct competitor** — it served a similar first customer and solved a similar job.
2. **Business-model analogue** — the product may differ, but pricing, sales motion, or delivery economics are comparable.
3. **Channel specialist** — it has demonstrated one acquisition channel you need to understand.

Write one sentence for each choice: “We selected X because, during its early stage, it sold Y to Z through model M.” If you cannot write that sentence with evidence, do not add the company to the working set.

Large brands are often poor starting references. Their current website reflects accumulated distribution, trust, and resources. A pre-PMF startup needs to study the version that existed before those advantages.

## How do you reconstruct a competitor's early strategy?

Website evolution is the sequence of changes in positioning, customer proof, pricing, product scope, and calls to action over time. Start with the official site, then use the [Internet Archive's Wayback Machine](https://web.archive.org/) to inspect dated versions.

Select three to five meaningful snapshots rather than every capture:

- first discoverable version;
- private beta or waitlist period;
- first version with a paid offer;
- first version with clear customer proof;
- current version.

For each snapshot, record the exact date, headline, primary CTA, target user, use cases, pricing signal, proof, navigation, community links, and any new SEO content structure. Save the source URL and screenshot. If a page is missing or unreadable, mark it unknown instead of filling the gap with a guess.

The goal is not to imitate the latest page. It is to identify the order in which the company narrowed its promise, proved demand, added commercial structure, and expanded distribution.

## What should go into the competitive analysis template?

The template below separates observed evidence from interpretation. Copy it into a spreadsheet or working document.

| Field | Observation | Interpretation | Confidence | Decision affected |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Snapshot date | Exact archive date | Why this stage matters | High/medium/low | Positioning |
| Hero statement | Verbatim headline | Job and audience implied | High/medium/low | Homepage copy |
| Primary CTA | Waitlist, try, buy, contact | Product-led or sales-led motion | High/medium/low | Conversion path |
| Pricing | Visible price or no price | Packaging hypothesis | High/medium/low | Offer design |
| Proof | Logos, quotes, usage numbers | Trust strategy | High/medium/low | Evidence plan |
| Use cases | Named jobs or segments | Wedge versus expansion | High/medium/low | Roadmap |
| Channels | Official social/community links | Where attention may compound | High/medium/low | Channel test |
| Content structure | Guides, comparisons, tools | Search investment stage | High/medium/low | SEO backlog |

Do not combine observation and judgment in one cell. “The homepage added a pricing link in March” is evidence. “The company had found product-market fit” is an interpretation that requires more proof.

## How do you analyze pricing and distribution without inventing causality?

Pricing and channel analysis should be framed as dated hypotheses. A visible price change proves that the page changed; it does not prove why revenue changed. A social icon appearing in the footer proves that the company linked the channel; it does not prove that the channel drove customers.

Use a three-column evidence rule:

| What you can verify | What you may infer | What you still need |
|---|---|---|
| Pricing changed from A to B on dated pages | Packaging became more productized | Revenue or conversion evidence |
| A community link appeared | Community became part of the journey | Member activity and referral data |
| A topic cluster was launched | Search became an acquisition bet | Ranking, traffic, and conversion data |

For traffic and channel estimates, preserve the provider, capture date, geography, and definition. Do not present a Similarweb estimate or SEO-tool estimate as first-party analytics. When the decision is expensive, cross-check with another source.

## How do you turn competitor research into a 14-day experiment?

A competitive analysis is finished only when it changes a decision. Convert each recommendation into a bounded experiment:

| Decision | Evidence behind it | 14-day action | Primary signal | Stop condition |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Narrow the homepage promise | Stage-matched pages lead with one job | Test one job-specific hero | Qualified CTA clicks | No relevant conversations |
| Test one channel | Comparable teams repeatedly use one channel | Publish and distribute 3 assets | Relevant replies or demos | Attention without ICP fit |
| Change the offer | Pricing history shows a simpler entry point | Interview 5 buyers on one package | Paid pilot intent | Objections point elsewhere |
| Add proof | Competitors foreground outcomes | Publish one evidence card | Assisted conversions | Evidence cannot be verified |

Assign an owner, review date, and disconfirming signal. “Post more on LinkedIn” is not an experiment. “Publish three teardown posts for B2B growth leads by November 24; continue only if they produce at least two relevant conversations” is testable.

## Which tools belong in this workflow?

Use tools according to the question, not because a dashboard is available.

- [Wayback Machine](https://web.archive.org/) for dated website versions.
- [Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/) for category discovery and launch artifacts.
- [Show HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/showhn.html) for technical and developer-product launches.
- [GitHub Trending](https://github.com/trending) for open-source discovery signals.
- [Analook](https://www.analook.com/) for a consolidated competitor-research starting point.
- [Gingiris competitor-research skill](https://tools.gingiris.com/skills/competitor-research/) for an operational research checklist.

Automated collection can accelerate the 0-to-60 work. It cannot decide whether two companies are truly comparable or whether an observed change caused a result.

## What are the most common competitive-analysis failures?

The first failure is selecting famous companies instead of relevant ones. The second is analyzing only the current homepage. The third is collecting screenshots without preserving dates and source URLs. The fourth is turning correlation into causality. The fifth is ending with a report instead of a decision.

Before sharing the analysis, run this final check:

1. Every material claim has a source and date.
2. Observations and interpretations are separated.
3. Unknowns remain visible.
4. Each recommendation names the evidence that supports it.
5. The next action has an owner, deadline, metric, and stop condition.

If the output cannot pass those five checks, it is still research notes—not a competitive strategy.

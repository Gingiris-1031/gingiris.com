A signed agreement does not mean a project is ready to start. Client onboarding converts a commercial promise into a working system: the right people, inputs, access, milestones, and decision rules are in place before delivery accelerates.

Iris Wei's self-reported consulting experience shows why this matters. Projects described as “growth problems” sometimes turned out to be activation, positioning, measurement, or decision-ownership problems. Starting with recommendations before diagnosing the constraint created rework.

## Client onboarding checklist

| Area | Confirm before work accelerates | Owner |
|---|---|---|
| Decision | What decision or outcome will this project support? | Executive sponsor |
| Scope | Deliverables, exclusions, versions, dates | Project leads |
| People | Sponsor, operator, approver, subject experts | Client lead |
| Inputs | Analytics, documents, access, interviews | Named provider |
| Baseline | Current metrics and definitions | Data owner |
| Cadence | Meetings, async updates, response windows | Both sides |
| Acceptance | Evidence, reviewer, correction process | Approver |
| Change | How new requests affect time and fees | Authorized leads |
| Commercial | Invoice trigger, expenses, pause conditions | Finance contacts |
| First milestone | Observable output in the first week | Delivery lead |

## 1. Restate the business decision

Ask the sponsor to complete one sentence: “At the end of this engagement, we need enough evidence to decide whether to ___.”

This is stronger than “improve growth.” It can be choosing a launch market, deciding whether onboarding is the bottleneck, approving a channel pilot, or defining a repeatable consulting offer.

If the answer differs from the proposal, resolve it before kickoff.

## 2. Translate the SOW into an operating map

Review every deliverable, exclusion, dependency, milestone, acceptance rule, and payment trigger. The [statement of work template](/en/insights/statement-of-work-template) provides the document structure; onboarding assigns people and dates to it.

Create a one-page operating map:

```text
Objective:
Deliverables:
Explicit exclusions:
Sponsor / operator / approver:
Required access and data:
Weekly cadence:
First milestone and date:
Acceptance evidence:
Change approvers:
Escalation path:
```

## 3. Name decision rights, not only attendees

A kickoff with twelve people can still have no owner. Identify who supplies data, who does daily work, who approves changes, who accepts delivery, and who resolves a blocked decision.

Do not assume the most senior attendee performs every role. Record the names in the project workspace.

## 4. Verify access and baseline definitions

“We have analytics” is not enough. Confirm the property, date range, event definitions, identity rules, source attribution, and known gaps. Missing data should be marked unavailable rather than converted into zero.

For qualitative evidence, identify customers, sales calls, support tickets, product recordings, prior experiments, and relevant decisions. Preserve source dates and definitions.

## 5. Agree on communication and response windows

Define where decisions live, where files live, which channel is urgent, how quickly each side normally responds, and what happens when an input is late. A chat group is not a project record unless decisions are summarized and stored.

Set a predictable rhythm: a weekly decision meeting, an async evidence update, and a visible risk log are often enough for a bounded project.

## 6. Create a first-week evidence milestone

The first milestone should reveal whether the project is pointed at the right problem. Examples include a verified funnel baseline, five customer evidence cards, a competitor decision map, or a launch-readiness audit.

Avoid spending the first week polishing a large final deck. Produce something that can change the plan cheaply.

## Red flags that should pause onboarding

- no decision-maker is available;
- the objective changes in every meeting;
- access cannot be granted and no alternative evidence exists;
- the client expects work that is explicitly out of scope;
- success depends on a metric nobody can define;
- billing or procurement ownership is unknown;
- identifiable customer evidence lacks permission.

Pausing here protects both parties. It is cheaper than discovering the same conflict halfway through delivery.

## Close onboarding with a readback

Ask the client lead to explain the objective, next milestone, owners, meeting rhythm, acceptance rule, and change process in their own words. Correct any mismatch in writing.

# Launch Budget Planning: How to Estimate KOL Costs

After four years of global launch work, one of the questions I hear most often is: **How much did that launch cost?**

You can often estimate the paid portion from the outside, but only if you stop counting mentions as if they were identical. A long thread, a standalone post, a quote post, and a simple repost are four different deliverables. YouTube, Instagram, and TikTok use different pricing logic again.

The goal is not to produce a magically precise number. It is to understand where the money probably went and what each layer was expected to do.

## Start by classifying the format

When a product appears across dozens of accounts, sort each mention first:

- long thread or narrative post;
- standalone post;
- quote post with commentary;
- repost without additional content;
- long-form video or dedicated review;
- organic mention with no confirmed payment.

Then add the variables that change the rate: audience geography, creator size, actual relevance, production work, review rounds, usage rights, exclusivity, and timing.

In some historical mid-tier negotiations, I saw bundles that combined a thread, standalone post, and quote post. I also saw long-form video quotes vary widely and US-focused creators charge more. These are dated samples, not a rate card. Reusing them as today's market price without checking current quotes would be misleading.

## A bundle is a scope decision, not automatically a bargain

Bundles can reduce the apparent cost per deliverable, but the real question is whether the pieces work together. A thread may explain the product, a short post may remind the audience, and a quote post may amplify a strong source asset.

Before agreeing, define:

| Field | Question |
|---|---|
| Audience | Does this creator reach the buyer or user we need? |
| Deliverable | What exactly will be published, and when? |
| Review | Who approves factual claims and product details? |
| Rights | Can the company reuse the content or paid media? |
| Measurement | Which link, code, or event can be checked? |
| Failure boundary | What happens if the post is late or inaccurate? |

## Follower count is not the buying criterion

I care more about whether an account feels alive: specific opinions, recognizable readers, real replies, and content that the creator could plausibly have written without a brief.

Large accounts can provide reach. Mid-sized creators can provide trust. Neither guarantees conversion. Check past sponsored work, comment quality, audience geography, suspicious spikes, and whether the creator can show the product in a real scenario.

A strong demo sometimes earns unpaid sharing. That does not make creator work free; it means the content itself created enough interest for someone to help voluntarily.

## Paid distribution is only one layer

A launch usually spreads through four different groups:

1. the internal team;
2. partners and people informed before launch;
3. paid creators and media;
4. users who choose to share.

Only the third layer belongs in the creator budget. The second layer is relationship work done before launch. The fourth can only happen if the product and story give people a reason to share.

This is why a campaign that looks like an overnight success may have required weeks of preparation. The visible posts are the output; the briefing, relationships, product proof, and source content came earlier.

## Reverse-engineer a launch without pretending certainty

To estimate a competitor's launch:

1. collect mentions during a defined launch window;
2. classify each mention by format and likely paid/earned status;
3. group accounts by audience and region;
4. use current quotes or documented ranges where available;
5. report a range and assumptions, not a single authoritative number.

For video, widen the window because production and publication often happen before or after the main launch day. Mark unknown sponsorship status as unknown.

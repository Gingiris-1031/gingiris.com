In my first 14 months operating independently, I reported cumulative revenue of roughly RMB 1 million. The headline is less useful than the sequence behind it: one showcase, referrals, stronger filtering, recurring advisory work, and a small support system around the founder.

| Stage | What you need | What you do not need yet | Evidence to collect |
|---|---|---|---|
| First proof | One showcase project | A full service catalog | Before/after, decision log, client quote |
| Repeatability | One narrow problem and buyer | More followers | Three similar paid conversations |
| Pricing | A clear decision and artifact | Perfect hourly math | Objections, close rate, delivery cost |
| Leverage | A stable delivery loop | A large full-time team | Repeated tasks and failure points |
| Compounding | Referrals and reusable assets | Constant channel switching | Lead source, conversion, retention |

## How do you get the first client without a big audience?

Do not begin by publishing ten service packages. Begin by creating one piece of proof that makes a buyer say, “I want that outcome.”

My first strong showcase came from a Product Hunt launch project. The project gave future clients something concrete to inspect: the constraints, the decisions, the launch sequence, and the result. The showcase did more work than a generic “growth consultant” description could.

A credible showcase should answer five questions:

1. What was the starting situation?
2. Which constraint mattered most?
3. What did you choose not to do?
4. What did you change?
5. What can a future client reasonably learn from the result?

One case is not a universal guarantee. A showcase demonstrates how you think under a specific set of conditions.

## Why should a consultant narrow ten problems to three?

Founders often bring ten simultaneous problems: positioning, acquisition, pricing, hiring, launch, partnerships, community, content, retention, and fundraising. A consultant who tries to execute all ten becomes an extra pair of hands. A consultant who identifies the three decisions that unlock the others becomes valuable.

The work is not “doing less” for the client. It is reducing decision cost. Use this filter in the first diagnostic conversation:

- Which problem changes the next 90 days if solved?
- Which decision is reversible, and which creates lock-in?
- Which problem is actually a symptom of another decision?
- Which action can produce evidence within two weeks?
- Which action should be explicitly postponed?

The output should be a three-item operating agenda, not a longer backlog.

## What are you really selling: time or judgment?

Time is how delivery is scheduled. Judgment is why a client pays.

I began with an uncertain hourly price, later moving from roughly RMB 800 per hour to RMB 1,500 per hour, and added a monthly advisory option around RMB 7,000. These are historical, self-reported prices from one practice—not a market benchmark.

The offer became clearer when it included a defined question, a decision the client needed to make, a preparation process, a concrete artifact after the call, and a boundary around what was not included.

If the deliverable is only “60 minutes with me,” buyers compare calendars. If it is “a decision memo that narrows your launch from ten options to three,” buyers can evaluate value.

## How can one person deliver without becoming the bottleneck?

Leverage should follow repeated demand. It should not hide an unclear offer.

My operating setup eventually included AI workflows, a part-time content lead, and administrative support. I kept the work that required context, trust, and judgment. Repetitive preparation, formatting, research collection, scheduling, and content repurposing moved into documented workflows.

| Keep with the founder | Systematize or delegate |
|---|---|
| Diagnosis and prioritization | Scheduling and reminders |
| Sensitive client conversations | Research collection |
| Final recommendations | Draft formatting |
| Pricing and scope decisions | Content repurposing |
| Relationship ownership | Status reporting |

AI can reduce the cost of repeatable 0-to-60 work. It does not remove responsibility for 60-to-90 judgment.

## What does a sustainable week look like?

A solo business needs protected thinking time. In the operating rhythm described in my founder deck, Monday was reserved for rest and core problems; Tuesday through Friday included two to four client calls plus skills and content work; weekends were used selectively for activities and workshops.

The exact calendar is not the lesson. The lesson is separation: delivery blocks, thinking blocks, asset-building blocks, and recovery blocks. If every day mixes client calls, sales, writing, and operations, the founder becomes the queue.

## Should a one-person business stay one person forever?

No. “One-person company” is a stage and an operating choice, not a moral identity.

The next step may be a small team, a productized service, software, or a platform. Hire when the bottleneck is a repeatable function with a stable standard—not when the founder is temporarily busy. Keep the parts that depend on trust and judgment close until they can be taught and reviewed.

## A seven-day starting checklist

1. Write down the ten problems clients currently bring you.
2. Circle the three where your judgment is unusually strong.
3. Choose one past result that can become a showcase.
4. Describe the offer as a decision and artifact, not a call length.
5. Ask three potential buyers which part is unclear.
6. Set one price and one scope boundary for the next five conversations.
7. Track where every lead came from and why it converted or did not.

If your company has too many growth options and no operating priority, [start with one focused advisory conversation](/en/services). The first output should be a decision map: what matters now, what can wait, and what evidence changes the next move.
